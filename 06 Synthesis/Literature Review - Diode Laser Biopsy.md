Literature Review Outline — Diode Laser Oral Soft-Tissue Biopsy Specimen Quality

Outline only, per instructions. No prose sections drafted. Built entirely from vault evidence already read; no new literature search performed.

1. Proposed title

"Diode Laser Operating Parameters, Delivered Power, and Incision Speed in Relation to Histopathologic Specimen Quality of Oral Soft Tissue: A Methodological Literature Review"

Deliberately names the causal chain (parameters → delivered power → speed → specimen quality) rather than claiming a result, and says "methodological" rather than "systematic" since the vault's own novelty passes are explicitly not a systematic review.

2. Major-section outline

1. Clinical and methodological importance of oral soft-tissue laser biopsy

Purpose: Establish why histopathologic specimen quality from laser-excised oral tissue is a real clinical concern, not an abstract technical question.
Connects from: Opens the review — no prior section.
Key claims:
- Oral soft-tissue lesions require histopathologic diagnosis for management decisions.
- Diode lasers are adopted clinically for hemostasis/visibility advantages, creating a known trade-off against thermal artifact.
- This review's own scope is the ex vivo experimental question, with clinical literature retained as context only.
Studies: Angiero 2011, Romeo 2014, Gambino 2026, Gundlapalle 2022, Shnawa 2025 (clinical adoption context); project framing itself (Diode Laser Biopsy.md).

2. Determinants of diode laser–tissue interaction

Purpose: Lay out the operating-parameter vocabulary (wavelength, mode, contact, tip status, fiber diameter) the rest of the review depends on.
Connects from: Section 1 established why artifact matters; Section 2 establishes what physically produces it.
Key claims:
- Wavelength determines chromophore absorption and penetration depth.
- CW vs. pulsed mode, duty cycle, contact/tip-initiation status are independent covariates, not interchangeable with power.
- These parameters must be tracked individually because studies vary more than one at a time.
Studies: Gutiérrez-Corrales 2020, Romanos 2022, Al-Ani 2023/2024, Merigo 2012, Gundlapalle 2022 (CW/PW).

3. Set power versus actual delivered power

Purpose: Establish that console setting ≠ delivered output, and that this gap is real, measured, and device-dependent.
Connects from: Section 2's parameters are meaningless if the "power" parameter itself is unreliable — this section undercuts the assumption that a reported wattage describes what tissue actually received.
Key claims:
- Console-set power does not reliably equal measured delivered output.
- The deviation is device-dependent in both magnitude and direction (not a uniform loss).
- Independently measured output has been incorporated into several oral/dental ex vivo studies.
Studies: Parker 2022, Xue 2022, Zegaib 2011, Kim 2020 (bench); Hanke 2021, Strakas 2023, Goharkhay 1999, Prado 2022, Merigo 2012 (tissue-paired).

4. Incision speed and exposure mechanics

Purpose: Establish incision speed as a controlled/reportable experimental variable with its own provenance taxonomy, analogous to Section 3's power treatment.
Connects from: Parallel structure to Section 3 — power and speed are the review's two controllable exposure variables; having separated set-vs-measured power, the review now separates reported-vs-controlled speed the same way.
Key claims:
- Incision speed has been mechanized, clinician-controlled, measured as an outcome, or left unreported across different sources — these are not interchangeable.
- Speed must never be derived from distance ÷ time when not explicitly reported.
- Only a handful of fixed speed values exist in the literature; none varies speed as an independent variable against outcome.
Studies: Hanke 2021, Strakas 2023, Goharkhay 1999 (mechanized); Isola 2018 (measured outcome); Al-Ani 2023/2024 (clinician-executed, reported); Romanos 2013 (illustrative sweep, review-tier).

5. Histopathologic effects of laser cutting

Purpose: Define and separate the distinct histologic constructs the rest of the review will use, preventing the single biggest source of cross-study confusion.
Connects from: Sections 3–4 established the exposure side (power, speed); Section 5 turns to the tissue-response side these exposures produce.
Key claims:
- Thermal injury magnitude (a linear/area measurement) is not the same construct as artifact morphology/severity (a categorical/ordinal judgment).
- Margin/incision condition is a third, partially overlapping construct.
- Terminology is heterogeneous across sources (TDE, LTDE, ETTD, TDD/TDA) but measures a related family of constructs.
Studies: Monteiro 2019, Vescovi 2010, Al-Ani 2024, Gundlapalle 2022, Azevedo 2016, Seoane 2013, Gobbo 2017, Pergolini 2025, Hanke 2021, Strakas 2023, Goharkhay 1999, Prado 2022.

6. Biopsy specimen quality and histopathologic interpretability

Purpose: Introduce the construct this project's outcome framework centers on — whether the whole specimen remains interpretable, distinct from any single thermal or artifact measurement.
Connects from: Section 5 established the component-level histologic constructs (A–C); Section 6 asks the composite question those components feed into — is the specimen still readable as a diagnostic-analog object.
Key claims:
- Specimen interpretability is a distinct construct from diagnosis rendered (a binary fact) and from any single thermal/artifact measurement.
- A structured ordinal slide-quality grading approach for this construct exists in the clinical literature (Gundlapalle's slide-quality scale) but not yet in an ex vivo design.
- Measurability and diagnosability can diverge within the same dataset.
Studies: Gundlapalle 2022, Gobbo 2017, Monteiro 2019, Vescovi 2010, Seoane 2013.

7. Specimen size and diagnostic vulnerability

Purpose: Establish specimen size as a confound that must be controlled in any ex vivo design, using the vault's clearest dose-response evidence.
Connects from: Section 6 established that specimens can fail to be interpretable; Section 7 identifies the single strongest known driver of that failure identified in the clinical literature — size relative to the thermal-damage zone.
Key claims:
- Small specimens are disproportionately vulnerable to edge artifact because thermal damage occupies a larger fraction of total tissue.
- Reported size thresholds are study/device/tissue-specific and must not be pooled into one universal cutoff.
- This has direct design implications for standardizing ex vivo specimen dimensions.
Studies: Angiero 2011, Vescovi 2010, Monteiro 2019 (explicit contrast — no size effect found).

8. Integration of measured power, speed, and histopathologic outcome evidence

Purpose: Show explicitly which pairwise and triple combinations of {measured power, controlled speed, histologic outcome, biopsy-oriented specimen-quality outcome} already have precedent, and which do not.
Connects from: Sections 3, 4, and 5–7 each established one axis in isolation; Section 8 is where the review actually cross-tabulates them against each other — the necessary step before the gap can be stated precisely.
Key claims:
- Measured power + histology in ex vivo oral tissue is already demonstrated (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay, Prado).
- Measured power + controlled speed + histology is already demonstrated (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay) — novelty cannot rest on this combination alone.
- Biopsy-oriented specimen-quality outcomes exist, but without measured power, in a separate stream (Al-Ani 2024, Gundlapalle, Pergolini partially).
Studies: Hanke 2021, Strakas 2023, Goharkhay 1999, Prado 2022, Al-Ani 2024, Gundlapalle 2022, Pergolini 2025.

9. Remaining methodological gap

Purpose: State the actual, narrow, evidence-supported gap — not a broad "no one has studied this" claim.
Connects from: Directly follows from Section 8's cross-tabulation; the gap is defined as the empty cell in that matrix.
Key claims:
- The gap is not "measured power + speed + histology" (closed) or "laser biopsy quality" (extensively studied clinically).
- The gap is the specific triple intersection: independently measured power + controlled/reported speed + biopsy-oriented specimen-quality outcome, in one ex vivo oral design.
- This is stated as "not identified in the reviewed evidence," not as an absolute claim.
Studies: Synthesis of all Section 8 studies; Novelty Matrix as the source document.

10. Rationale for the present study

Purpose: Bridge from the identified gap to the present study's specific design choices.
Connects from: Section 9 identified the gap; Section 10 explains why the present study's design is the logical response to it.
Key claims:
- Ex vivo oral tissue permits independent power/speed control impossible in a clinical setting.
- The outcome framework must go beyond thermal damage alone, per Section 5–6's construct separation.
- Diagnostic yield is explicitly out of scope for a non-lesional ex vivo model.
Studies: Synthesizes the whole review; no new citations, only argument closure.

4. Claim → evidence → caution → transition (per subsection)

(Given the volume, this section covers every subsection at the depth of the Task 4 example. Organized by major section.)

1.1 Diagnostic necessity of oral soft-tissue histopathology

CLAIM: Definitive management of oral soft-tissue lesions depends on histopathologic diagnosis of the excised specimen.
BEST SUPPORT: Angiero 2011, Romeo 2014, Gambino 2026 (all render a pathologist diagnosis as the study endpoint).
CAUTION: This is background/context, not evidence about laser-specific artifact.
TRANSITION: Because diagnosis depends on specimen readability, the excision method used to obtain the specimen becomes methodologically relevant.

1.2 Laser excision adoption and rationale

CLAIM: Diode lasers are used for oral soft-tissue excision because of intraoperative hemostasis and visibility advantages over scalpel.
BEST SUPPORT: Shnawa 2025, Gundlapalle 2022 (both frame laser use around these clinical advantages).
CAUTION: Adoption rationale is not evidence that specimen quality is preserved — that is a separate empirical question addressed later.
TRANSITION: These advantages come with a known trade-off — thermal energy delivery — which is the review's central mechanistic concern from here forward.

1.3 Hemostasis/visibility benefit vs. thermal-artifact risk

CLAIM: The same thermal energy that provides hemostasis also produces artifact capable of degrading histologic interpretation.
BEST SUPPORT: Gundlapalle 2022 (thermal necrotic border vs. slide quality), Monteiro 2019 (diode's highest epithelial score among six instruments).
CAUTION: Neither study establishes that this trade-off is unmanageable — several sources report preserved diagnosis despite measurable artifact.
TRANSITION: Managing this trade-off requires understanding what physically determines the magnitude of thermal delivery — the subject of Section 2.

1.4 Scope boundary: ex vivo focus vs. clinical literature

CLAIM: This review treats ex vivo experimental evidence as its primary target and clinical diagnostic-biopsy evidence as contextual only.
BEST SUPPORT: Project framing itself (Diode Laser Biopsy.md's 2026-09-13 reframing).
CAUTION: This boundary must be maintained explicitly in prose, since most processed clinical sources report rich histologic detail that could otherwise dominate the argument.
TRANSITION: With scope fixed, the review turns to the parameter vocabulary needed to describe laser-tissue interaction precisely.

2.1 Wavelength and chromophore absorption

CLAIM: Wavelength determines which tissue chromophore (melanin, hemoglobin, water) preferentially absorbs energy, shaping penetration depth and damage pattern.
BEST SUPPORT: Gutiérrez-Corrales 2020 (six wavelengths, 810 nm at 0.5–2 W identified as least damaging in that model), Al-Ani 2024 (dual 810+980 nm vs. 2780 nm comparator).
CAUTION: Wavelength-damage relationships are model-specific (in vitro periodontal pocket vs. ex vivo sheep tongue) and should not be pooled into one ranking.
TRANSITION: Wavelength alone does not determine tissue effect — operating mode is an independent covariate, addressed next.

2.2 CW vs. pulsed mode and duty cycle

CLAIM: CW and pulsed operation produce different, and currently inconsistent, thermal-damage directions across sources.
BEST SUPPORT: Gundlapalle 2022 and Romanos 2022 (PW < CW damage); Pergolini 2025 (PW > CW, non-significant); Goharkhay 1999 (no consistent direction).
CAUTION: This is a confirmed unresolved conflict, not resolvable by this review — must be presented as open, not averaged.
TRANSITION: Mode interacts with contact status and tip condition, the next parameters requiring separate tracking.

2.3 Contact/non-contact and tip-initiation status

CLAIM: Tip-initiation status is treated as a deliberately controlled variable in some designs but is rarely varied against a measured outcome.
BEST SUPPORT: Al-Ani 2023/2024 (initiated tip), Gutiérrez-Corrales 2020 (non-initiated, deliberate isolation), Romanos 2022 (initiated vs. non-initiated comparison, but measures penetration depth not power, and carries an internal abstract-vs-table direction inconsistency).
CAUTION: No source pairs a controlled initiated-vs-non-initiated comparison with independently measured power — Prado 2022 has measured power but no non-initiated arm.
TRANSITION: Fiber diameter and beam-delivery geometry modify how any given power/mode combination is actually delivered at the tissue surface.

2.4 Fiber diameter, beam-delivery geometry, and irradiated area

CLAIM: Fiber diameter and beam-delivery geometry influence the effective irradiated area and therefore the local irradiance associated with a given delivered power; fiber diameter alone is not equivalent to power density, and studies vary this parameter without always reporting it.
BEST SUPPORT: Al-Ani 2024 (400 μm), Vescovi 2010 (320 μm), Gundlapalle 2022 (unreported — flagged UNKNOWN).
CAUTION: Missing fiber-diameter data in several sources (Gundlapalle, Romeo partial) limits irradiated-area/irradiance comparability across the review's evidence base, and beam-delivery geometry (tip shape, divergence, working distance) is generally unreported even where fiber diameter is known.
TRANSITION: Having established the full parameter set, the review can now isolate the single parameter most central to reproducibility: power itself.

2.5 Why parameters must be tracked as independent covariates

CLAIM: No single parameter (power, mode, wavelength, tip status) can be interpreted in isolation without confounding the others.
BEST SUPPORT: Cross-source pattern across all of 2.1–2.4.
CAUTION: This is a methodological principle, not itself an empirical finding requiring citation-by-citation support.
TRANSITION: Section 3 begins the parameter-by-parameter treatment with power, the most commonly reported but least reliably comparable variable.

3.1 Console setting ≠ delivered output

CLAIM: Console-set power does not reliably represent actual power delivered at the fiber output.
BEST SUPPORT: Parker 2022, Xue 2022, Zegaib 2011, Kim 2020 — every source reporting both values found a nonzero difference at every device × setting tested.
CAUTION: Device-specific output deviation cannot be generalized across all diode platforms — no two of these four sources share a device model.
TRANSITION: Because delivered power differs from console setting, the direction and cause of that difference must be characterized before power can be treated as a controlled variable.

3.2 Sources and direction of output deviation

CLAIM: Deviation between set and measured power is device-dependent in both magnitude and direction, not a uniform loss.
BEST SUPPORT: Kim 2020 (one device +11.9–18.1%, two devices −13.9 to −27.3%, all under one shared protocol); Xue 2022 (mixed-sign by wavelength/mode); Parker 2022 and Zegaib 2011 (predominantly below-panel).
CAUTION: Kim 2020's within-study result argues the direction is a device property, but this is the source's own interpretation, not independently re-derived.
TRANSITION: This bidirectional, device-specific pattern is what makes independent measurement in any given tissue-effect study non-optional.

3.3 Evidence from independently measured diode output in tissue-paired studies

CLAIM: Several ex vivo oral-tissue studies have already paired independently measured delivered power with a histologic outcome.
BEST SUPPORT: Hanke 2021 and Strakas 2023 (per-incision QC verification), Goharkhay 1999 (wattmeter at fiber outflow), Prado 2022 (PM600 meter, numeric measured values reported), Merigo 2012 (pooled measured loss figure, not diode-isolated).
CAUTION: None of these four/five pairs measured power with a biopsy-oriented specimen-quality outcome — this precedent closes the power-measurement question, not the project's actual gap.
TRANSITION: Power measurement alone does not determine tissue effect — the speed at which the fiber traverses tissue is an equally necessary, equally under-verified variable.

3.4 Consequences for reproducibility of tissue-effect studies

CLAIM: Any tissue-effect study reporting set power only cannot be assumed comparable to another study at the "same" nominal wattage.
BEST SUPPORT: Cross-reference of Section 3.1–3.3 findings against the clinical sources in Sections 6–7 (Angiero, Romeo, Gambino, Azevedo — all set-power-only).
CAUTION: This is an inference from the power-deviation literature applied to the biopsy literature, not independently tested within any single biopsy study.
TRANSITION: This same reproducibility problem applies to incision speed, addressed next.

4.1 Speed as a controlled experimental variable

CLAIM: Incision speed is a plausible exposure determinant through its effect on local dwell/exposure conditions per unit tissue, and must be tracked with the same rigor as power; direct oral-tissue evidence experimentally isolating speed's effect on outcome remains sparse.
BEST SUPPORT: Hanke 2021/Strakas 2023 (mechanized 2 mm/s, mechanistic rationale via fixed dwell time, not a varied-speed experiment), Romanos 2013 (illustrative speed-damage relationship, review-tier).
CAUTION: Romanos 2013 is a non-empirical CE review — its speed-damage claim is illustrative, not primary data; Hanke/Strakas report a single fixed speed rather than varying it, so neither source directly, experimentally isolates speed's effect on oral-tissue outcome.
TRANSITION: Because speed can be produced by different mechanisms, its provenance must be classified, not just its numeric value recorded.

4.2 Provenance taxonomy

CLAIM: A reported speed value's control mechanism (mechanized, clinician-controlled, measured-as-outcome, descriptive, unknown) materially affects its comparability across studies.
BEST SUPPORT: Hanke/Strakas (mechanized, micropositioner); Isola 2018 (measured outcome, Sinc metric, distinct from a separate clinician-estimated target); Al-Ani 2023/2024 (reported value, control mechanism unstated → unknown despite being numeric).
CAUTION: A reported number alone never justifies "mechanized" or "clinician-controlled" — most oral-biopsy sources with a numeric speed (Al-Ani) fall into "unknown" by this taxonomy.
TRANSITION: Applying this taxonomy across the vault's evidence shows how sparse genuinely controlled speed data actually is.

4.3 Reported speed values across oral/dental diode literature

CLAIM: Only a small set of fixed speed values exists in the reviewed literature (10, 2, 1, 0.75, 0.61 mm/s), each from a single source/protocol.
BEST SUPPORT: Goharkhay 1999 (10 mm/s), Hanke/Strakas (2 mm/s), Li 2022 (1 mm/s), Al-Ani 2023/2024 (0.75 mm/s), Isola 2018 (0.61 mm/s measured outcome).
CAUTION: These values are not mutually comparable — different tissues, devices, and control mechanisms underlie each.
TRANSITION: No source varies speed as an independent variable against outcome — this absence becomes one input to the gap argument in Section 9.

4.4 Absence of speed varied as an independent variable

CLAIM: No reviewed source manipulates incision speed across multiple levels against a specimen-quality or thermal outcome.
BEST SUPPORT: Cross-tabulation of all Section 4.3 sources — each reports one fixed value.
CAUTION: This is an absence within the reviewed evidence, not a claim that no such study exists anywhere.
TRANSITION: With power and speed both characterized as under-verified exposure variables, the review turns to what these exposures actually produce in tissue.

5.1 Thermal injury magnitude as a distinct construct

CLAIM: Thermal injury magnitude — a linear or area measurement of how far/much thermal alteration extends — is a distinct, quantifiable construct across sources despite inconsistent naming.
BEST SUPPORT: Monteiro 2019 (TDE), Al-Ani 2024 (LTDE), Prado 2022 (TDD/TDA — area+depth), Hanke/Strakas (TIZ), Azevedo 2016 (ETTD).
CAUTION: Measurement methods differ (magnification, staining, instrument) across sources — magnitudes are broadly comparable in order only, not directly poolable.
TRANSITION: Magnitude alone does not describe what kind of cellular change occurred — that is a separate, categorical construct.

5.2 Artifact morphology/severity as a distinct construct

CLAIM: Artifact morphology (epithelial/connective-tissue changes, carbonization, pseudodysplastic artifact) is a categorical/ordinal judgment distinct from thermal magnitude.
BEST SUPPORT: Monteiro 2019 and Vescovi 2010 (fused epithelial/connective 0–3 sum-scores), Azevedo 2016 (0–4 carbonization scale), Seoane 2013 (pseudodysplastic artifact, the vault's most specific artifact-mimicry construct).
CAUTION: Monteiro's and Vescovi's own sum-scores already fuse artifact-type and architecture sub-items — this construct cannot always be cleanly isolated even within one source.
TRANSITION: A third construct — the condition of the cut edge itself — is reported separately from both magnitude and artifact severity in several of these same sources.

5.3 Margin/incision condition as a distinct, partially overlapping construct

CLAIM: Incision regularity/edge quality is reported as its own ordinal outcome, separate from thermal magnitude and artifact severity.
BEST SUPPORT: Vescovi 2010 (0–4 incision-quality score, later reused by Monteiro), Monteiro 2019 (0–4 regularity-of-incision score), Azevedo 2016 (incision regularity %).
CAUTION: This construct legitimately overlaps with thermal magnitude when the same μm value serves both purposes (e.g., Romeo's margin-distance figures) — overlap is expected, not an error, but must not be collapsed into one field.
TRANSITION: Having separated these three component constructs, the review can now state precisely why cross-study terminology is heterogeneous rather than treating it as sloppy reporting.

5.4 Terminology heterogeneity and why constructs are kept separate here

CLAIM: Different named metrics (TDE, LTDE, ETTD, TDD/TDA) measure a related family of thermal-magnitude constructs but are not interchangeable without verification.
BEST SUPPORT: Cross-source naming comparison across 5.1–5.3.
CAUTION: This review does not normalize this terminology into one unified construct — per explicit instruction, heterogeneous source terminology is preserved, not merged.
TRANSITION: Beyond these component-level constructs lies a composite question the rest of the review's outcome argument depends on — whether the whole specimen remains interpretable.

6.1 Specimen interpretability distinct from diagnosis rendered

CLAIM: Whether a specimen is judged readable/interpretable is a distinct construct from whether a diagnosis was in fact rendered.
BEST SUPPORT: Gobbo 2017 (20/93 specimens with unmeasurable thermal damage, yet diagnosis unimpaired in every case — the clearest demonstration that these constructs diverge).
CAUTION: Most sources that state "diagnosis was not limited" (Monteiro, Vescovi) do not add a distinct interpretability judgment beyond that binary fact — this must not be over-read as evidence of a separate graded construct in those two sources specifically.
TRANSITION: Where a source does grade interpretability explicitly, rather than just stating a binary outcome, a structured grading approach becomes visible — the subject of the next subsection.

6.2 Structured slide-quality grading approaches in the literature

CLAIM: A structured ordinal slide-quality grading approach for specimen interpretability exists in the clinical literature (pathologist-graded slide quality) but has not been applied in an ex vivo design.
BEST SUPPORT: Gundlapalle 2022 (1=good→5=non-diagnostic, scored at periphery and center, PW graded better than CW).
CAUTION: This instrument is in vivo, human, set-power-only — it cannot be imported into an ex vivo design without independent validation there.
TRANSITION: Gobbo's measurability-vs-diagnosability divergence and Gundlapalle's graded instrument together motivate treating specimen interpretability as its own outcome domain, addressed next.

6.3 Divergence between measurability and diagnosability

CLAIM: A specimen can have thermal damage too extensive or too subtle to measure quantitatively while still permitting successful diagnosis.
BEST SUPPORT: Gobbo 2017 (21.5% unmeasurable, 0% diagnosis-impaired).
CAUTION: This is a single-source finding — no other reviewed source reports an equivalent measurability-vs-diagnosability split with this level of granularity.
TRANSITION: This divergence is exactly why a binary diagnostic-yield endpoint is insufficient for the present study's design goals.

6.4 Why diagnostic yield cannot substitute for a specimen-quality framework

CLAIM: Diagnostic yield, as a binary or percentage outcome, does not capture the graded interpretability information the present study needs.
BEST SUPPORT: Synthesis of 6.1–6.3; Angiero 2011 (yield is size-graded but reported only as a threshold percentage, not a continuous quality gradient).
CAUTION: This is an argument about outcome-framework adequacy, not a criticism of the diagnostic-yield studies themselves, which serve a different (clinical) purpose.
TRANSITION: One of the strongest known drivers of diagnostic-yield failure — specimen size — deserves its own focused treatment before the review moves to integration.

7.1 Size-graded diagnostic failure in a large clinical series

CLAIM: Diagnostic failure rate is sharply size-graded in at least one large clinical series.
BEST SUPPORT: Angiero 2011 (63.63% unreadable at 2 mm, 37.71% at 3 mm, 0% at ≥4 mm; n=608).
CAUTION: Single-institution, retrospective; the 2/3/4 mm cutoffs are this study's own categories, not externally validated boundaries.
TRANSITION: A second, independent source finds a related but not identical size effect, using a different design.

7.2 Size-dependent artifact frequency independent of power

CLAIM: Epithelial/stromal thermal changes are significantly more frequent in specimens below a size threshold, independent of laser power or frequency.
BEST SUPPORT: Vescovi 2010 (specimens <7 mm, p<0.0001, across two Nd:YAG power/frequency settings).
CAUTION: Vescovi's is a non-diode (Nd:YAG) source and uses a 7 mm cutoff, not Angiero's 4 mm — these are not the same threshold and must not be merged.
TRANSITION: A third source, using the review's own diode evidence, illustrates why no single threshold should be adopted.

7.3 Why thresholds are study-specific, not universal

CLAIM: Reported size thresholds (Angiero's 4 mm, Vescovi's 7 mm) are specific to their own device, tissue, and specimen-handling protocol and must not be pooled into one recommended cutoff.
BEST SUPPORT: Monteiro 2019's explicit contrast — no size-outcome correlation was found in its own sample (P=0.661 epithelial, P=0.288 connective), attributed by the source itself to its sample not including specimens as small as Angiero's or Vescovi's.
CAUTION: Monteiro's null finding is evidence of a genuine study-level boundary condition, not evidence against a size effect existing in general.
TRANSITION: Because no universal threshold can be claimed, ex vivo specimen dimensions must be designed conservatively rather than borrowed directly from any one clinical threshold.

7.4 Implications for standardizing ex vivo specimen dimensions

CLAIM: Ex vivo specimen dimensions should be standardized a priori and chosen so that specimen size does not itself dominate thermal-edge or specimen-quality outcomes, using the clinical size-effect literature as design context rather than as a source of a directly transferable cutoff.
BEST SUPPORT: Synthesis of 7.1–7.3 as design context — the clinical size-effect literature (Angiero, Vescovi, Monteiro's null) motivates controlling size as a standardized design variable, not a borrowed numeric threshold.
CAUTION: This is a design inference drawn by this review, not a claim any single source makes directly; no universal minimum specimen size is derived here, since reported thresholds are study/device/tissue-specific (7.3).
TRANSITION: With exposure variables (Sections 3–4), outcome constructs (Sections 5–6), and the size confound (Section 7) all separately established, the review can now cross-tabulate them against each other.

8.1 Measured power + histology precedent

CLAIM: Independently measured diode-laser power paired with histologically assessed tissue effect is already demonstrated in ex vivo oral tissue.
BEST SUPPORT: Hanke 2021, Strakas 2023, Goharkhay 1999, Prado 2022.
CAUTION: All four are cutting-efficiency/thermal-characterization studies, not biopsy-oriented — this precedent does not by itself address specimen quality.
TRANSITION: Three of these four sources additionally control incision speed, closing a second link in the chain.

8.2 Measured power + speed + histology precedent

CLAIM: Measured power, controlled incision speed, and histologic outcome are already jointly demonstrated in oral ex vivo tissue.
BEST SUPPORT: Hanke 2021 and Strakas 2023 (identical protocol, 2 mm/s mechanized, per-incision power verification); Goharkhay 1999 (wattmeter-measured output, mechanized 10 mm/s, histologic outcome).
CAUTION: Novelty cannot be claimed from this triple combination alone — it already has direct prior art across three sources, not just Hanke/Strakas.
TRANSITION: What Hanke/Strakas/Goharkhay do not provide is a biopsy-oriented outcome framework — a separate literature stream approximates that instead.

8.3 Biopsy-oriented outcomes without measured power

CLAIM: Ordinal, biopsy-adjacent specimen-quality outcomes exist in ex vivo and clinical settings, but without independently measured power.
BEST SUPPORT: Al-Ani 2024 (ordinal ET/CT scores + LTDE, ex vivo, confirmed setup-check-only power), Gundlapalle 2022 (graded slide quality, in vivo, set-power-only), Pergolini 2025 (crossed power×mode design, but continuous not ordinal outcome, no measured power).
CAUTION: None of these three closes the measured-power gap; each approximates a different piece of the missing outcome framework.
TRANSITION: These two evidence streams — Section 8.1/8.2's measurement rigor and Section 8.3's outcome richness — have not yet been combined in one design.

8.4 Why these remain separate, incompletely integrated streams

CLAIM: The measured-power/speed stream and the biopsy-oriented-outcome stream are evidenced by non-overlapping sets of studies.
BEST SUPPORT: Direct comparison of 8.1–8.2's study list against 8.3's study list — zero shared sources.
CAUTION: This is a structural observation about the reviewed evidence base, not itself a novelty claim — that follows in Section 9.
TRANSITION: This structural gap is now precise enough to state as the review's central methodological gap.

9.1 Restating the integration failure precisely

CLAIM: The reviewed evidence contains two separately well-developed streams (measured power/speed rigor; biopsy-oriented outcome richness) that have not been integrated in one ex vivo oral design.
BEST SUPPORT: Section 8 in full.

7. Histopathologic construct map

A. Thermal injury magnitude — TDE, LTDE, TDD/TDA, TIZ/TDZ, tissue modification width. Linear/area measurements. Sources: Monteiro, Al-Ani 2024, Prado, Hanke/Strakas, Azevedo, Seoane, Gobbo (pooled, not compartment-split).

B. Artifact morphology/severity — epithelial changes, connective-tissue changes, carbonization, vacuolization, coagulation, pseudodysplastic artifact. Categorical/ordinal judgments. Sources: Monteiro, Vescovi (fused sum-scores), Azevedo (carbonization scale), Seoane (pseudodysplastic construct), Gundlapalle (unscored artifact list).

C. Margin/incision condition — incision regularity, margin readability, edge quality. Sources: Vescovi, Monteiro (0–4 regularity scores), Azevedo (regularity %).

D. Specimen interpretability — slide-quality grading, correct histological sampling, diagnostic interference/readability. Sources: Gundlapalle (graded scale), Gobbo (measurability-vs-diagnosability split), Seoane (interpretive dysplasia-interference conclusion).

E. Diagnostic outcome — diagnosis rendered, diagnosis affected, diagnostic yield. Sources: Angiero, Romeo, Gambino, Palaia, Isola, Shnawa, Monteiro, Vescovi, Gobbo, Gundlapalle (all as a binary/percentage fact).

These constructs overlap but are not interchangeable. A/C overlap when the same μm value is both a thermal-magnitude and a margin-distance measurement (Romeo, Al-Ani's LTDE). B and D overlap when an artifact list (B) is what a source cites as evidence for its interpretability conclusion (D) (Seoane). B and the vault's separately-tracked "tissue architecture" field are frequently fused by source papers themselves (Monteiro's and Vescovi's sum-scores mix nuclear/cytoplasmic change with loss-of-attachment in one number) — this review does not attempt to un-fuse what the source itself reported as one value. E is the coarsest construct and must never be treated as a proxy for D — Gobbo's 20/93 finding is the clearest proof these two diverge.

8. Gap argument (cumulative, 7 steps)

1. Actual delivered power can differ from set power, and the deviation is device-dependent in direction and magnitude (Parker, Xue, Zegaib, Kim).
2. Independently measured power has been incorporated into several oral soft-tissue technical studies, including ex vivo diode-tissue designs (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay, Prado, Merigo).
3. Incision speed has also been controlled or reported in several of these same studies, using a mix of mechanized, measured-outcome, and reported-but-unverified provenance (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay, Isola, Al-Ani).
4. Histologic thermal-effect outcomes are well established across both ex vivo and clinical sources, using a heterogeneous but related set of named metrics (Monteiro, Vescovi, Al-Ani, Azevedo, Prado, Seoane).
5. Biopsy-oriented specimen-quality outcomes — including a structured ordinal slide-quality grading approach — also exist in a separate clinical/histopathology literature (Gundlapalle, Al-Ani 2024, Gobbo, Pergolini partially).
6. These evidence streams are incompletely integrated: the sources with the strongest measurement rigor (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay, Prado) use cutting-efficiency or non-biopsy-oriented outcomes, while the sources with the richest biopsy-oriented outcomes (Gundlapalle, Al-Ani 2024) lack independently measured power.
7. The current vault has not identified a study combining rigorous measured delivered power, controlled/reported incision speed, and biopsy-oriented specimen-quality outcomes in a standardized ex vivo oral soft-tissue model — this appears under-integrated in the reviewed evidence and remains incompletely addressed, not "never studied" or "first ever."

9. Final present-study rationale outline

1. Why ex vivo oral tissue — permits independent power/speed control unavailable in a clinical design (10.1).
2. Why independently measured delivered power — console setting is demonstrably unreliable at the device level (10.2, grounded in Section 3).
3. Why controlled/reported incision speed — no reviewed source varies speed as an independent variable; provenance must be explicit (10.3, grounded in Section 4).
4. Why set power and measured power must be kept as separate reported values — collapsing them would reproduce the exact ambiguity Section 3 identifies as a reproducibility failure across the field.
5. Why the histopathologic outcome must go beyond thermal damage alone — thermal magnitude, artifact severity, margin condition, architecture preservation, and specimen interpretability are demonstrably separable constructs that can diverge from one another (Section 5–6, especially Gambino's and Gobbo's dissociation findings).
6. Why objective thermal-damage metrics should be paired with structured specimen-quality/interpretability outcomes — magnitude alone does not predict interpretability; a graded instrument analogous to Gundlapalle's, adapted for ex vivo use, closes a documented instrument gap (6.2, 9.3).
7. Why diagnostic yield itself should not be claimed — no real lesion exists in a standardized ex vivo model; diagnostic yield remains clinical/translational context only (10.5).
8. Logical bridge to the research question — these design choices converge directly on: how diode laser operating parameters, actual delivered power, and incision speed affect histopathologic quality of ex vivo oral soft-tissue specimens (10.6).

No hypothesis is included — Diode Laser Biopsy.md states a research question and rationale but no explicit hypothesis statement, so none is invented here per instruction.
