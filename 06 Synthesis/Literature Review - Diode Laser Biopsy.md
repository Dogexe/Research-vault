5. Histopathologic Effects of Laser Cutting

Before specimen quality can be assessed as a whole, this review separates the histopathologic constructs the literature actually reports. Three partially overlapping but non-identical constructs recur across the diode and comparator-laser sources: the spatial magnitude of thermal alteration, the categorical morphology of that alteration, and the physical condition of the incised margin. Treating a micrometer measurement, an ordinal artifact score, and an edge-regularity judgment as interchangeable evidence of "how good the cut was" is a common source of cross-study confusion, avoided deliberately below.

5.1 Thermal injury magnitude as a distinct construct

Thermal injury magnitude — how far, how deep, or how much tissue alteration extends from a laser incision — is in principle a directly measurable spatial quantity. In practice, the reviewed sources operationalize it in materially different ways, and the resulting values are not simply interchangeable numbers on one scale.

Several sources report a single linear distance from the incision edge to the limit of visible thermal change, each under its own name. Monteiro et al. (2019) named this "Tissue Damage Extension" (TDE), reported separately for epithelium and connective tissue, perpendicular to the surgical margin (diode arm: epithelial 913.73 ± 322.45 μm, connective 284.81 ± 110.56 μm). Al-Ani et al. (2024) reported a conceptually similar but independently named "lateral thermal damage extent" (LTDE), without Monteiro's epithelial/connective split (diode arms averaging 221.24 ± 85.44 μm). Azevedo et al. (2016) used a third named metric, "Extent of Thermal Tissue Damage" (ETTD), reporting 456.15 μm at a standard pulsed diode setting and 626.82 μm at a higher "Boost" setting on the same device. Seoane et al. (2013), using a non-diode Er,Cr:YSGG laser, measured an analogous edge-to-damage width but found a substantially smaller magnitude (mean 26.60 ± 25.3 μm) in a different tissue and device context — illustrating that superficially similar linear measurements may not be directly comparable across different laser systems, tissues, and measurement protocols.

Other sources depart from a single linear figure. Prado et al. (2022) reported both a total area (mm²) and a depth (μm) of thermal damage as separate axes, without an epithelial/connective split. Hanke et al. (2021) and Strakas et al. (2023) defined a "total interaction zone" (TIZ) depth as the denominator of a cutting-efficiency ratio in a non-lesional, ex vivo cutting-behavior study — not a margin-quality outcome reported for its own sake. Gobbo et al. (2017) reported a single pooled "maximum thermal damage along the cutting margin" with no epithelial/connective split, and — an inconsistency preserved here rather than corrected — labeled this linear measurement a "thermal damage score" in one table header despite it not being ordinal by the paper's own description.

Measurement location differs correspondingly: perpendicular distance into adjacent tissue (Monteiro, Vescovi), lateral extent to the nearest normal tissue (Al-Ani), or an unspecified margin-wide maximum (Gobbo). None of these sources reports inter-rater reliability, and most rely on a single assessor. Given these differences in named metric, compartment splitting, measurement geometry, and tissue/device model, this review does not pool thermal-magnitude values numerically; the constructs are related by family, not interchangeable by value. Dimensional outcomes are methodologically attractive because they are reproducible and instrument-verifiable, but a linear or area measurement says nothing on its own about whether the kind of change that occurred would interfere with interpretation — the construct addressed next.

5.2 Artifact morphology and severity as a distinct construct

Where thermal magnitude asks how far alteration extends, artifact morphology and severity ask what kind of change occurred and how disruptive it is — a categorical or ordinal judgment rather than a continuous one. Recurring features across the reviewed sources include nuclear and cytoplasmic alteration, loss of epithelial or connective-tissue attachment, carbonization, vacuolization, coagulation-related change, and, in one source, artifact that mimics dysplastic change.

Monteiro et al. (2019) and Vescovi et al. (2010) each constructed an ordinal epithelial score and connective-tissue score (0–3 each) by summing binary sub-items — nuclear/cytoplasmic change and loss of attachment for the epithelial score; carbonization, desiccation, and vascular change for the connective-tissue score. Monteiro's criteria were explicitly adopted from Vescovi's original scheme — a confirmed lineage, not an independent invention — and neither source reports its sum-score as validated: both used a single blinded pathologist with no inter-rater reliability statistic given. Because these scores are constructed within the source papers as a fused sum of sub-items, this review does not retroactively decompose them into separate constructs; they are treated as the single ordinal variables their authors defined. Azevedo et al. (2016) scored carbonization on its own 0–4 scale and found, within the diode arm specifically, that the higher-ETTD "Boost" setting produced a lower carbonization score (1.80) than the standard setting (2.50), even though a pooled ETTD–carbonization correlation held across that study's full six-instrument comparison. This within-device pattern is reported descriptively here and should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal inverse relationship between ETTD and carbonization.

Gundlapalle et al. (2022) reported a descriptive, unscored list of histologic artifacts in diode-laser biopsy specimens — necrosis, hyperchromatism, nuclear elongation, hyalinization, vacuolation, epithelium–connective-tissue separation — functioning as a qualitative inventory rather than a graded construct, used here only in that capacity. Seoane et al. (2013) introduced the most distinct construct in this group: "pseudodysplastic artifact," a pattern of thermally induced cellular/nuclear polymorphism, hyperchromatism, and loss of intercellular adherence that can be mistaken for genuine epithelial dysplasia rather than merely representing generic damage. This is kept separate from ordinary artifact severity because the risk it names is qualitatively different — not that the specimen is damaged, but that the damage can be misread as a different, more consequential diagnosis — and it was assessed via a blinded, two-pathologist consensus process, the strongest pathologist-involvement design in the reviewed evidence for this question.

A further caution against overstating causal attribution comes from Romeo et al. (2014), whose discussion notes that many artifacts observed in their laser-excised specimens were attributable to standard fixation, sectioning, and staining processing rather than to the laser incision itself. None of the ordinal systems surveyed here — Vescovi's, Monteiro's, or Azevedo's — is described by its source as validated in a psychometric sense; each is a study-specific construction scored by a single blinded assessor, and this review does not upgrade that status without direct support. Even so defined, artifact severity still does not describe the physical condition of the cut edge — the construct addressed next.

5.3 Margin and incision condition as a distinct, partially overlapping construct

Margin or incision condition concerns the regularity and physical quality of the cut boundary — how cleanly the laser divided the tissue — rather than how far thermal change extended (5.1) or what cellular changes occurred (5.2). Vescovi et al. (2010) introduced a 0–4 incision-quality score (4 = scalpel-like regularity), later reused by Monteiro et al. (2019) as its own "regularity of incision" score on the same 0–4 scale. Azevedo et al. (2016) reported a related but differently structured measure — the percentage of incisions classified regular versus irregular (20% regular at both tested diode settings) — rather than a continuous score.

That margin condition is related to, but not identical with, thermal magnitude is shown by Monteiro et al.'s own finding of a statistically significant, moderate correlation between incision-regularity score and TDE (epithelial r = −0.438, connective r = −0.467, both p < 0.001): less regular incisions tended to accompany greater thermal-damage extent, but this is a correlation between two separately measured variables, not evidence of one construct. Romeo et al. (2014) and Al-Ani et al. (2024) each report margin-distance data — Romeo's per-lesion-type margin damage (roughly 0.15–0.62 mm across diode-treated lesion types) and Al-Ani's observation that no group's LTDE exceeded 0.3 mm — illustrating how the same physical measurement can serve more than one construct at once: a distance value can function both as evidence of thermal magnitude and as the empirical basis for a prospective safety-margin recommendation. This overlap is expected, not an inconsistency, but it does not make the constructs identical.

Two further distinctions matter. First, a recommended safety-margin width — additional healthy tissue several sources advise excising beyond a lesion, such as Al-Ani et al.'s suggested 0.5–1 mm — is a forward-looking design recommendation derived from a measured thermal-damage value. Accordingly, this review treats recommended safety-margin width and observed margin readability as distinct constructs. Second, an incision-regularity score describes only the cut edge, not the interpretability of the specimen as a whole; Gobbo et al.'s finding — discussed further in Section 6 — that thermal damage was unmeasurable in over a fifth of specimens without diagnostic impairment shows that whole-specimen readability is governed by more than edge regularity alone. With thermal magnitude, artifact morphology, and margin condition established as related but non-identical constructs, the terminology describing them can now be examined directly.

5.4 Terminology heterogeneity and implications for cross-study interpretation

The preceding subsections already show a broader pattern: the literature uses a heterogeneous, only partially standardized vocabulary for closely related outcomes. Named or semi-named metrics encountered here include TDE (Monteiro), LTDE (Al-Ani et al. 2024), ETTD (Azevedo), TDD/TDA (Prado), TIZ (Hanke; Strakas), an unnamed "overall width of tissue modifications" (Vescovi), a "necrotic border width" (Gundlapalle), an unnamed "width of thermal damage at the incision edge" (Seoane), and a "maximum thermal damage" value that one source's own table header inconsistently also calls a "thermal damage score" (Gobbo) — alongside a separate family of margin-condition labels: incision quality, incision regularity, and regularity-of-incision scores (Vescovi, Monteiro, Azevedo).

Similar labels do not guarantee identical protocols. Monteiro et al.'s "TDE" illustrates this: the underlying epithelial/connective evaluation criteria were adopted from Vescovi et al. (2010), but the linear "TDE" abbreviation itself was not inherited — it is Monteiro and colleagues' own naming for a measurement built on borrowed criteria. Conversely, differently named metrics can still belong to a related family without being directly poolable: TDE, LTDE, ETTD, and Vescovi's unnamed width measurement are all, broadly, linear thermal-magnitude measurements near an incision margin, but differ in compartment splitting, measurement geometry, and tissue/device context (5.1). TIZ stands further apart: despite describing a comparable zone of altered tissue, it was defined within a cutting-efficiency framework in a non-lesional, non-biopsy model, not as a biopsy-margin outcome, and should not be read as equivalent to the TDE/LTDE/ETTD family without that context.

This review therefore preserves each source's own terminology rather than collapsing these labels into one umbrella term such as "thermal damage," and maintains the three-way separation established above: (1) thermal injury magnitude, a spatial/area measurement of how far or how much alteration extends; (2) artifact morphology and severity, a categorical or ordinal judgment of what kind of change occurred and how severe it is; and (3) margin/incision condition, a judgment of the cut boundary's physical regularity. These constructs are related — they frequently co-occur in the same specimen and are sometimes derived from overlapping measurements — but they are not interchangeable, and none of them, individually or combined, has been shown here to fully determine whether a specimen remains interpretable as a whole. That composite question — specimen interpretability — is a further, distinct construct addressed in Section 6.

## 3. Set Power Versus Actual Delivered Power

The exposure side of this review's causal chain begins with the power value an operator sets or reads from a laser console. Before delivered power, incision mechanics, and tissue interaction can be compared across studies, this section asks whether that console value can itself be trusted as a description of what tissue actually received — first from bench and device-level evidence on the relationship between set and measured power (3.1–3.2), then from oral soft-tissue studies that have already measured power independently (3.3), before drawing out the methodological implications for the tissue-effect literature the rest of this review depends on (3.4).

### 3.1 Console setting versus delivered output

A power value selected on a laser's control panel, or shown on its display, is a device setting rather than a verified physical description of delivered exposure. Several bench-level studies in this vault test directly whether the two coincide. Parker et al. (2022) measured post-fibre emission power for 38 semiconductor diode dental laser units spanning 20 device types (445–1064 nm), at control-panel settings of 100 mW, 500 mW, 1.0 W, and 2.0 W, and found statistically significant variation in the percentage deviation between panel and measured output, both between devices and between emission-power levels; the study reports device-specific values rather than one study-wide figure. Xue et al. (2022), testing two dental devices (445/970 nm and 810 nm) in continuous-wave and pulsed modes, likewise measured output that differed from the preset value at every device and mode tested — but not in one direction: continuous-wave mean deviations were +21%, +22%, and −8% across the three wavelengths, and pulsed deviations were +37%, +26%, and −3%. Zegaib et al. (2011), studying a single 810 nm unit under a periodontal-curettage protocol, measured output below its 1.0 W panel reference in every tested condition, with a mean 9% fibre-transmission loss before tissue contact. Kim et al. (2020) measured both a set ("Output Power") and an independently measured ("Actual Power") value for three separate dental diode devices — K2 mobile, Epic 10, and Saeshin — at matched 1.0/2.0/3.0 W settings, using one calibrated power meter and one shared fibre-tip type, and found the two values differed at every one of the nine device-by-setting combinations tested.

Together, these four sources establish that some degree of disagreement between set and measured output occurs across the evaluated devices, but they do not support the stronger claim that diode lasers "lose power" as a rule. Xue et al. and Kim et al. each report output above the preset value for at least one device. The reviewed evidence does not support applying a single correction factor across devices. A nominal wattage displayed on a console is a device setting, not necessarily a complete physical description of the exposure a specimen receives. If the relationship between displayed and measured power varies across devices, the pattern of that variation must itself be examined before it can be reasoned about.

### 3.2 Device-dependent power deviation

The same four sources show that measured-versus-set deviation is heterogeneous rather than uniform. Kim et al. (2020) is the clearest demonstration because it holds the measurement protocol constant: under one calibrated power meter and one shared fibre tip, the K2 mobile device measured consistently above its panel setting (+11.9% to +18.1%), while the Epic 10 and Saeshin devices measured consistently below theirs (−13.9% to −27.3%). Because this mixed-direction result occurs within a single study using a shared protocol, it weakens the alternative explanation that the opposite-direction findings of Parker et al. (predominantly below-panel output) and Xue et al. (mixed-sign output) simply reflect differing measurement setups across separate studies — Kim et al. reproduces the same directional conflict on its own. Zegaib et al.'s single-device result adds a below-panel data point rather than resolving this conflict, and its design further shows the magnitude of deviation is not fixed even within one device: fibre output fell from a mean 9% loss before tissue contact to a mean 40.26% loss by the fourth contact application in the study group, against a 0.14–1.58% loss range in a non-contact control condition. Within Zegaib’s experimental setup, repeated use and contact condition were associated with progressive output loss, indicating that output deviation may vary even within a single device protocol. Parker et al.'s own device-specific table values, spanning 20 device types, are not reducible to one aggregate figure either, and this review does not pool them into one.

Because no two of these sources share a common device, protocol, or calibration standard, this review does not average their percentages into one cross-device deviation estimate; direction and magnitude of deviation are treated as device-level (and, per Zegaib et al., condition-level) properties, not as a single correction to be applied elsewhere. Because console values are device-dependent proxies rather than fixed offsets, studies that independently verify actual output provide a stronger characterization of tissue exposure than console values alone.

### 3.3 Independently measured power in oral soft-tissue studies

Independent power verification has already been incorporated into ex vivo oral soft-tissue studies that also assess histologic tissue effects, including studies that additionally control incision speed. Hanke et al. (2021) measured emitted power at the distal fibre end with a calibrated power meter before every individual cut across eight diode wavelengths (405–1470 nm, 0.5–4 W set), using a ≥5% deviation threshold to trigger fibre-tip replacement — a per-incision verification protocol, not a single setup check — but the study does not tabulate the resulting measured wattage as a value separate from the nominal set power; only the set-power axis appears as the reported independent variable. Incision speed in this design was mechanized and constant (2 mm/s, via a computer-controlled micropositioner), and histologic outcomes (cut depth, cut width, total interaction zone depth) were assessed on porcine gingiva; no diagnosis was rendered and no lesion was excised. Strakas et al. (2023) extends this same protocol — same power meter, same ≥5% threshold, same micropositioner, same 2 mm/s speed — to 940 nm across nine power levels (1–6 W), with the same caution: independent per-incision verification occurred, but no separately tabulated measured-wattage value is reported.

Goharkhay et al. (1999) is numerically distinct: output was measured directly at the fibre outflow with a wattmeter, and the resulting values (0.5–4.5 W with a 400 μm tip; 0.5–2 W with a 200 μm tip) are the study's own reported "average power" figures — the source states explicitly that these are measured, not console, values, and does not report a separate panel setting at all. Incision speed was mechanized and constant at 10 mm/s, and histologic outcomes (incision depth, vertical and horizontal thermal-damage width) were measured on ex vivo pig mandible mucosa. Goharkhay et al. is accordingly an important precedent for the joint combination of measured power, controlled speed, and histology — a combination also demonstrated, without a separately tabulated wattage, by Hanke et al. and Strakas et al.

Prado et al. (2022) independently measured output with a power meter before each surgical procedure and reports both values numerically: a 1.2 W display setting measured 1.0 W, and a 1.8 W display setting measured 1.5 W, each roughly a 17% under-delivery. Histologic thermal-damage area and depth were assessed on ex vivo pig tongue. Incision speed was not reported in this design; cutting was performed manually, and only elapsed excision time — not a mm/s or cm/s speed — was recorded, and no speed value is inferred here where the source itself does not report one. Merigo et al. (2012) checked output with a power meter across five laser types, including one diode arm (808 nm, 3 W and 5 W), and found a pooled power loss of 18–25% "for all the used laser devices" — but this figure is not broken out per device, so the diode's individual deviation cannot be isolated from the pooled range; incision-quality scoring (criteria adapted from Vescovi et al.) was performed, but any diode-specific conclusion beyond the pooled loss figure would outrun what the source itself supports. Wilder-Smith et al. (1995) is included only as non-diode methodological precedent: using three CO2 lasers, it measured actual power (1, 4, and 12 W) with a calibrated meter before each incision and paired this with thermocouple-measured thermal events and histologic incision/damage measurements in pig mandible oral mucosa — precisely the measurement design largely absent from the diode literature — but its devices do not overlap with any diode source in this vault, so it cannot itself supply diode-specific evidence.

Across these sources, the combination of measured power and histologic assessment is already established prior art in ex vivo oral soft-tissue research, and the further combination of measured power, controlled or reported incision speed, and histology is also already established, principally through Hanke et al., Strakas et al., and Goharkhay et al. Neither combination is claimed as novel here. The methodological issue this review pursues is therefore not whether measured power has ever been used in oral soft-tissue laser research, but how exposure characterization affects the reproducibility and interpretation of tissue-effect studies more broadly — including the many studies that report set power only.

### 3.4 Implications for reproducibility and interpretation

Several biopsy- and histology-oriented studies already present in this vault report set power without independent verification: Angiero et al. (2011), Romeo et al. (2014), Azevedo et al. (2016), Gambino et al. (2026), and Gundlapalle et al. (2022) each report a console value with no power-meter check described. Al-Ani et al. (2023, 2024) occupy an intermediate position: a power meter (PINTUDY) was used to standardize the set value via the fibre cut, but the source does not describe this as independently re-verifying delivered output after setup, so it does not itself contribute a measured-versus-set deviation value.

Given the pattern established in 3.1–3.3, a set-power value reported in one of these studies cannot automatically be assumed to represent the same delivered exposure as a nominally identical set-power value reported in a different study on a different device — the deviation literature shows this relationship is device-dependent in both direction and magnitude, not a fixed offset. This creates genuine exposure uncertainty when comparing tissue-effect findings across studies that report set power only, and independent measurement, where available, improves characterization of the exposure a specimen actually received. Accordingly, this review treats set-power-only studies as informative but less precisely characterized with respect to delivered exposure. Their diagnostic-yield and histologic-scoring findings do not depend on knowing the delivered wattage to be interpretable in their own right — nor does this characterization demonstrate how much, if at all, histologic damage would have differed had delivered power been measured in any one of them; no source in this vault tests that counterfactual. Measured power also does not by itself resolve comparability across studies: wavelength, emission mode, fibre diameter and geometry, tip-initiation and contact condition, tissue characteristics, and incision speed all remain independent sources of variation regardless of how precisely power is characterized.

The distinction to preserve is between direct evidence and methodological inference. It is direct evidence, demonstrated by the bench and device-level studies of 3.1–3.2, that set and measured power diverge, and by how much, for the specific devices tested. It is a methodological inference, not independently tested within any single biopsy or histology study, that unverified nominal power therefore introduces uncertainty when tissue-effect findings are compared across devices. This review treats the inference as a caution for interpretation, not as direct proof that any particular study's reported histologic outcome would have differed under independent verification. Even when delivered power is characterized, tissue exposure is also influenced by the rate at which the fiber is advanced through tissue.

## 4. Incision Speed and Exposure Mechanics

### 4.1 Why incision speed matters mechanistically

The rate at which a laser fiber tip is advanced through tissue is mechanistically plausible as a determinant of local energy exposure: slower advancement plausibly increases the dwell time, and therefore the local exposure, delivered to each unit length of tissue, while faster advancement plausibly reduces it. This is a mechanistic rationale rather than, by itself, proof of the magnitude or direction of any histologic effect in oral tissue — local exposure is co-determined by delivered power, emission mode, fiber geometry, contact mode, tissue properties, and operator technique, not by speed alone. Romanos (2013), a single-author continuing-education review rather than a primary study, states this relationship most directly for diode lasers relying on the hot-tip cutting mechanism, via its own illustrative comparison (a five-point tested speed sweep from 12.5 to 0.0 mm/s, using a programmable translation stage). Romanos 2013 provides qualitative contextual support that slower advancement may increase thermal effect, but the evidence is non-primary and not sufficient to quantify an oral-tissue speed–response relationship. This statement is qualitative — no numeric thermal-damage-width values, stated sample size, or significance test accompany it, and the source does not confirm the tissue tested was oral — and it is used in this review only as mechanistic/context material, not as quantified or primary oral-tissue evidence. Direct oral-tissue evidence isolating speed as an independent variable is sparse.

Most oral soft-tissue studies that report speed use it as a controlled procedural parameter rather than as the experimentally varied factor.

### 4.2 Studies with controlled or reported incision speed

Where incision speed is reported in this vault's oral soft-tissue literature, it is most often held constant to standardize the experimental protocol rather than varied to test its own effect. Hanke et al. (2021) and Strakas et al. (2023) each mechanize incision speed at a constant 2 mm/s via a computer-controlled micropositioner, alongside independently verified delivered power (Section 3) and histologic assessment of cut depth, cut width, and thermal-interaction-zone extent in porcine gingiva. In both studies speed is held constant across all tested power levels rather than itself varied to estimate a speed-response relationship, so neither can be read as a speed-isolation experiment. Goharkhay et al. (1999) similarly mechanizes incision speed, at a constant 10 mm/s via a motorized handpiece, and pairs this with wattmeter-measured delivered power (0.5–4.5 W) and histologic measurement of incision depth and thermal-damage width in ex vivo pig mandible mucosa. Because Goharkhay et al. combines a controlled/reported speed, independently measured power, and a histologic outcome in one design, it remains an important precedent for that triple combination, but this combination alone does not establish an independent causal effect of speed, since speed itself was not varied within the study.

Isola et al. (2018) illustrates why speed values from different sources must not be merged even within one paper. Two distinct speed quantities are reported: an operator target of 2.5 mm/s, set by visual estimation during pyogenic-granuloma excision, and a separately defined measured outcome, "speed of incision" (surgical-field perimeter divided by incision time, timed with a chronometer), which the source itself treats as its primary result and which this vault records as the populated value — 0.61 ± 0.29 mm/s. The 2.5 mm/s visual target and the 0.61 mm/s measured outcome are not reconciled by the source and are preserved here as distinct quantities; only the latter is classified as measured in this vault's schema. Al-Ani et al. (2023, 2024) report a numeric 0.75 mm/s incision speed as a fixed operating parameter, but the control mechanism generating that value is not described, so it is classified as reported with unknown control rather than mechanized or measured. Li et al. (2022), in a mouse oral-mucosa wound model, similarly reports a fixed 1 mm/s diode tip-movement speed without a stated control mechanism, held constant against a comparator laser rather than varied. These distinctions — mechanized, measured, and reported-with-unknown-control — are kept separate throughout this review rather than collapsed into a single "speed was reported" category.

Standardizing speed can reduce procedural variability, but it does not reveal how outcomes change when speed itself changes.

### 4.3 Evidence for a speed–tissue effect relationship

None of the oral soft-tissue sources reviewed above varies incision speed as an independent experimental factor against a histologic or specimen-quality outcome; each reports one speed value (or, for Al-Ani and Li, one fixed value) held constant across its comparisons. The one source in this vault that does vary speed across multiple levels is Romanos (2013)'s illustrative five-point sweep — but this evidence is qualitative only, non-primary (a single-author review with no stated sample size or statistical test), and not confirmed to involve oral tissue at all; it cannot substitute for a controlled oral-tissue speed-response study, and this review does not treat it as one. No fixed-speed study reviewed here (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay, Isola, Al-Ani, Li) can be used to infer a dose-response relationship between speed and thermal injury. Within the reviewed oral-tissue evidence, no study identified here systematically varied incision speed while sufficiently constraining the other major exposure parameters to estimate an independent speed–response relationship. No correlation across these otherwise-unrelated studies — different devices, tissues, and species — is treated here as evidence of a speed effect, since each varies too many other conditions simultaneously to isolate speed's own contribution.

The reviewed literature supports the methodological importance of controlling or reporting incision speed more strongly than it supports a quantitatively established oral-tissue speed–response relationship.

This distinction matters because unreported or poorly controlled speed can still obscure interpretation even when power is known.

### 4.4 Implications for exposure characterization and reproducibility

Incision speed is part of exposure characterization alongside delivered power: identical delivered power can plausibly produce different local exposure histories if the rate of advancement differs, since dwell time per unit tissue length is not fixed by power alone. Where speed is unreported — as in Prado et al. (2022) and Merigo et al. (2012), both discussed in Section 3 for their measured-power evidence — this adds uncertainty when comparing tissue-effect findings across studies, on top of the set-versus-measured-power uncertainty already established there. Controlled or measured speed, where reported, improves reproducibility by removing one source of unstated variation between nominally similar procedures, but measured power without speed still leaves exposure incompletely characterized, and speed without measured power — as in Isola et al. (2018), Al-Ani et al. (2023/2024), and Li et al. (2022), none of which independently verifies delivered power — leaves it incompletely characterized in the other direction. Neither variable alone is sufficient to characterize the exposure a specimen received.

It is direct evidence, established in 3.1–3.3 and 4.1–4.2, that some studies measure or control speed, and that a smaller subset (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay) combines controlled speed with measured power and histology. It is a methodological inference, not itself tested within any single study reviewed here. Accordingly, this review treats unreported incision speed as a source of exposure uncertainty when nominally similar procedures are compared across studies. This review does not claim that speed necessarily explains all between-study differences in histologic outcome, that slower cutting always causes worse histology, that faster cutting is always safer, or that any one speed value is a universally optimal threshold — no source reviewed here supports such a claim. Even when delivered power and incision speed are characterized, their biological relevance must ultimately be judged through the histopathologic alterations produced in the tissue.

## 8. Integration of Measured Power, Incision Speed, and Histopathologic Outcome Evidence

### 8.1 Measured power and histology are already established

Independent power measurement has already been combined with histologic assessment of tissue effect in ex vivo oral soft-tissue research; this combination is not something this review can claim as unaddressed. Hanke et al. (2021) and Strakas et al. (2023) verify emitted power at the fiber with a calibrated power meter before every incision, replacing the fiber tip whenever deviation reaches 5%, and pair this verification with histologic assessment of cut depth, cut width, and thermal-interaction-zone extent — though, as established in Section 3, neither study tabulates the resulting measured wattage as a value distinct from the nominal set power. Goharkhay et al. (1999) reports a numerically explicit measured-power range (0.5–4.5 W, wattmeter-verified at the fiber outflow) alongside histologic measurement of incision depth and thermal-damage width, making it the clearest single-source precedent for numerically reported measured power paired with histology. Prado et al. (2022) independently measured output before each procedure and reports both display and measured values numerically (a consistent ~17% under-delivery), pairing this with histologic thermal-damage area and depth; incision speed is not reported in this design and is not invented here. Merigo et al. (2012) checked output with a power meter across five laser types including one diode arm, but the resulting loss figure (18–25%) is pooled across device types rather than diode-isolated, and its incision-quality scoring should not be read as diode-specific beyond that pooled figure. Independent power measurement in oral soft-tissue histology is therefore prior art, established across a small but real set of sources.

The literature also contains examples where measured power and controlled/reported speed were characterized together.

### 8.2 Measured power + speed + histology are also established prior art

The same three sources that anchor 8.1's clearest measured-power evidence — Hanke et al. (2021), Strakas et al. (2023), and Goharkhay et al. (1999) — also each combine independently measured or verified power, explicit speed control or reporting, and histologic evaluation in one design. Hanke et al. and Strakas et al. hold incision speed constant (2 mm/s, mechanized via a computer-controlled micropositioner) across every power level tested, so speed functions in both studies as a controlled procedural parameter rather than a varied experimental factor (Section 4); the triple combination is present, but neither study isolates speed's own contribution to the histologic outcomes reported. Goharkhay et al. provides a particularly clean version of the same precedent: both measured output (0.5–4.5 W) and incision speed (10 mm/s, mechanized) are reported as explicit numeric values alongside histologic measurement, without the tabulation gap present in Hanke and Strakas. None of the three reports a biopsy-oriented specimen-quality outcome — margin readability, tissue architecture, and specimen interpretability are not assessed in any of them, and this review does not present them as biopsy-quality frameworks. The combination of measured power, speed characterization, and histology is therefore already established in the reviewed evidence, at least at the level of cutting-efficiency and thermal-interaction outcomes.

What differs is the type of histopathologic outcome used to judge tissue quality.

### 8.3 Biopsy-oriented specimen-quality studies form a different outcome stream

A separate group of studies in this vault centers its outcomes on biopsy specimen quality rather than cutting behavior, and these studies generally define richer, more pathology-oriented constructs than the measured-power/speed studies of 8.1–8.2. Monteiro et al. (2019) and Vescovi et al. (2010) report thermal injury magnitude as a quantified linear distance (Tissue Damage Extension, by compartment), artifact morphology as an ordinal epithelial/connective-tissue sum-score, and margin/incision condition as a separate 0–4 regularity score — three constructs kept distinct within the same source. Gundlapalle et al. (2022) reports a structured ordinal slide-quality grading approach (periphery and center, graded 1 to 5) that speaks directly to specimen interpretability, a construct none of the measured-power/speed studies in 8.1–8.2 addresses at all. Gobbo et al. (2017) demonstrates that thermal injury magnitude and specimen interpretability can diverge within one dataset: 21.5% of its specimens had thermal damage too extensive or too subtle to measure, yet diagnosis was not impaired in any of them — a direct demonstration that a measurable-damage limitation does not equate to an interpretability or diagnostic failure. Seoane et al. (2013) introduces a construct not present elsewhere in this vault, pseudodysplastic artifact, and concludes that this specific artifact pattern did not interfere with distinguishing artifact from genuine dysplasia at the margin — an interpretive conclusion about diagnostic risk, not a numeric score. Angiero et al. (2011) and Vescovi et al. (2010) each reported greater vulnerability of smaller specimens within their own study-specific contexts, but their size thresholds and experimental settings were not equivalent and should not be interpreted as a shared cutoff.

Romeo et al. (2014) and Al-Ani et al. (2024) each report margin-distance data — Romeo's per-lesion-type histologic margin damage and Al-Ani's lateral thermal damage extent, none exceeding 0.3 mm — illustrating that a distance measurement can serve both as a thermal-magnitude figure and as the empirical basis for a margin recommendation, without itself being a readability score. Azevedo et al. (2016) contributes a thermal-magnitude figure (Extent of Thermal Tissue Damage) and a separate 0–4 carbonization scale alongside an incision-regularity percentage, again keeping magnitude, artifact severity, and margin condition as distinct reported values rather than one fused metric. None of these nine sources independently measures delivered power in the sense established in 8.1 — Al-Ani et al.'s power meter, as established in Section 3, was used only to standardize the set value at setup, not to re-verify delivered output, and this review does not imply otherwise. Across this stream, thermal magnitude, artifact severity, margin condition, and specimen interpretability recur as distinguishable constructs, and diagnostic outcome — whether a diagnosis was ultimately rendered — functions as a further, coarser construct that these sources generally keep separate from all of the above; Gobbo's divergence between measurability and diagnosis is the clearest demonstration that interpretability is not itself equivalent to diagnostic outcome.

The two literature streams therefore overlap in histology but emphasize different dimensions of exposure characterization and outcome quality.

### 8.4 The current evidence is complementary but methodologically disconnected

One stream — Hanke et al., Strakas et al., Goharkhay et al., and, more partially, Prado et al. and Merigo et al. — provides the stronger exposure characterization: independently measured or verified delivered power, controlled or numerically reported incision speed, and a histologic tissue-interaction outcome. A second stream — Monteiro et al., Vescovi et al., Gobbo et al., Seoane et al., Gundlapalle et al., Angiero et al., Romeo et al., Azevedo et al., and Al-Ani et al. (2024) — provides richer biopsy-oriented pathology outcomes: margin condition, artifact severity, specimen interpretability, and diagnostic relevance. The limitation this review identifies is not the absence of either stream on its own; both are represented in this vault by multiple sources. The limitation is that the two streams are not well aligned with each other. The measured-power/speed stream is built around cutting-efficiency and thermal-interaction outcomes rather than biopsy-readability outcomes — none of Hanke, Strakas, or Goharkhay assesses margin condition, specimen interpretability, or diagnostic relevance. The biopsy-quality stream, conversely, generally reports richer pathology outcomes while relying on nominal set power (Angiero, Romeo, Azevedo, Gundlapalle) or, at most, a power meter used for setup standardization rather than independent re-verification (Al-Ani). Accordingly, this review treats the separation between these evidence streams as a limitation in exposure–outcome integration rather than as evidence of bias or invalidity in either stream. The reviewed literature is therefore better characterized by complementary evidence streams than by a single integrated methodological framework.

### 8.5 Implications for study design

This integration pattern suggests what a design linking the two streams would need to characterize together. A stronger design would characterize independently measured delivered power, incision speed, and key delivery variables such as emission mode, fiber geometry, and tip-initiation/contact status, alongside biopsy-relevant histopathologic outcomes of the kind developed in 8.3 — margin condition, tissue architecture, and specimen interpretability, not thermal-damage magnitude alone. None of these elements is individually sufficient. Measured power alone, as Section 3 establishes, leaves speed and the remaining delivery variables uncharacterized. Controlled speed alone, as Section 4 establishes, leaves power uncharacterized. Thermal-damage width alone, as 8.3 shows through Gobbo et al.'s and Seoane et al.'s findings, does not by itself indicate whether a specimen remains interpretable. Diagnostic success alone is a clinical, real-lesion outcome that a non-lesional ex vivo design cannot claim. A design that links exposure characterization with biopsy-relevant histopathologic outcomes within one controlled protocol would permit more direct evaluation of exposure–outcome relationships. This integration problem leads directly to the methodological gap examined in Section 9.

## 9. Remaining Methodological Gap

### 9.1 What is already established

The individual methodological components this review has examined are not, on their own, unaddressed in the reviewed literature. Hanke et al. (2021), Strakas et al. (2023), and Goharkhay et al. (1999) each already combine independently measured or verified delivered power, controlled or explicitly reported incision speed, and histologic assessment of tissue effect. Prado et al. (2022) independently measures delivered power and pairs it with histologic thermal-damage outcomes, without a reported incision speed. Biopsy-oriented specimen-quality work — margin condition, artifact severity, specimen interpretability, and diagnostic relevance — is also already represented across Monteiro et al. (2019), Vescovi et al. (2010), Gobbo et al. (2017), Seoane et al. (2013), Gundlapalle et al. (2022), Angiero et al. (2011), Azevedo et al. (2016), Romeo et al. (2014), and Al-Ani et al. (2024). The individual methodological components are not novel.

The unresolved issue lies in how these components are combined.

### 9.2 The unresolved integration gap

Within the studies identified in this review, no ex vivo oral diode-laser study was found that simultaneously combined independently measured delivered power, controlled or explicitly reported incision speed, and a biopsy-oriented histopathologic specimen-quality outcome framework. This is not a claim of absence for any one of these elements — each is separately established, as 9.1 restates — but a claim about their joint occurrence within a single study design. The gap identified here is one of integration, not absence.

The measured-power/speed evidence in this vault (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay, and, more partially, Prado and Merigo) tends to emphasize thermal-interaction or cutting-efficiency outcomes rather than biopsy-oriented readability outcomes. The biopsy-quality evidence, conversely, tends to report richer pathology-oriented outcomes while relying on nominal set power or, at most, setup-stage power verification rather than independent re-confirmation of delivered output. Accordingly, the reviewed evidence provides limited direct integration between characterized delivered exposure and biopsy-oriented specimen-quality endpoints. This does not indicate that either stream of prior studies is methodologically inadequate for the purpose it was designed to serve; each addresses its own stated aim. It indicates only that the specific combination of exposure characterization and biopsy-oriented outcome assessment, within one ex vivo oral diode-laser design, is not represented in the evidence this review has identified.

### 9.3 Why this gap matters methodologically

The importance of this gap is methodological rather than merely descriptive. A console-set power value is not necessarily equivalent to the power actually delivered to tissue, and the deviation is device-dependent rather than uniform; a study relying on set power alone therefore leaves the exposure side of its own design incompletely characterized. Incision speed contributes to local exposure alongside power, so a design that measures power without reporting or controlling speed leaves the same characterization incomplete in a different respect. On the outcome side, thermal-injury magnitude, specimen interpretability, and margin condition are distinguishable constructs that can diverge within the same dataset — Gobbo et al.'s finding that unmeasurable thermal damage did not impair diagnosis is the clearest illustration. This review therefore treats thermal-injury magnitude as one component of specimen quality rather than as a complete surrogate for it. Specimen interpretability and margin condition are themselves distinct from whether a diagnosis was ultimately rendered. Linking exposure variables and biopsy-oriented pathology outcomes within one design would allow more direct interpretation of the relationship between characterized exposure and specimen quality than either evidence stream currently permits on its own; this is a methodological point about what a study design can support, not a claim about clinical or diagnostic improvement.

### 9.4 Scope of the gap

This gap statement is deliberately narrow, and several broader claims should not be read into it. It is not a claim that no laser-biopsy study has assessed specimen quality — the biopsy-oriented evidence stream demonstrates the opposite. It is not a claim that measured power has not been used in prior diode or oral soft-tissue research — Sections 3 and 8.1 establish that it has. It is not a claim that incision speed has not been controlled or reported previously — Sections 4 and 8.2 establish that it has, in the same three sources (Hanke, Strakas, Goharkhay) that already combine it with measured power and histology. It is, correspondingly, not a claim that measured power, speed, and histology together are a novel combination. Nor is this a systematic-review-level proof of global absence: the conclusion is bounded by the evidence identified in this review, by variation in how the reviewed sources name and operationalize related constructs, and by incomplete reporting in some primary studies — for example, several sources verify power without tabulating the measured wattage, or report speed without describing its control mechanism.

### 9.5 Methodological opportunity

A design responsive to this gap would integrate independently measured delivered power, controlled or explicitly reported incision speed, and relevant laser-delivery variables — emission mode, fiber geometry, and tip-initiation/contact status — with biopsy-oriented histopathologic outcomes such as thermal injury magnitude, margin/incision condition, tissue architecture where assessable, and specimen interpretability or readability. Diagnostic outcome, consistent with the scope boundary already established for a non-lesional ex vivo model, would remain contextual rather than the primary endpoint. Addressing this integration gap would allow the relationship between characterized laser exposure and biopsy-relevant histopathologic specimen quality to be examined within a single controlled experimental framework. The methodological gap provides the rationale for the design of the present study.

## 6. Biopsy Specimen Quality and Histopathologic Interpretability

### 6.1 From tissue alteration to specimen quality

Thermal injury magnitude, artifact morphology/severity, and margin/incision condition — the three constructs separated in Section 5 — each describe a real and measurable dimension of what a laser incision does to tissue, but none of them, individually or combined, is equivalent to whether the resulting specimen remains histopathologically usable and interpretable as a whole. A linear thermal-magnitude figure describes how far alteration extends; an ordinal artifact score describes what kind of cellular change occurred; a margin-regularity score describes the physical condition of the cut edge. Monteiro et al. (2019) and Vescovi et al. (2010) report all three of these component measures within the same design — thermal-damage extension, an epithelial/connective-tissue artifact sum-score, and an incision-regularity score — without treating any one of them, or their combination, as a stated specimen-quality verdict; the source papers report these as separate values rather than fusing them into an overall judgment. Azevedo et al. (2016) similarly reports a thermal-magnitude figure, a carbonization scale, and an incision-regularity percentage as three distinct values. Romeo et al. (2014) reports margin-distance data by lesion type without a corresponding specimen-quality or interpretability judgment. Specimen quality, as this review uses the term, is therefore broader than any single one of these measures, and this review treats specimen interpretability or readability as a distinct construct only where a source explicitly reports it as such — not as something inferable from a thermal-magnitude or margin-regularity value alone.

Some studies move beyond local damage metrics and explicitly judge the specimen or slide as a whole.

### 6.2 Structured assessment of slide or specimen interpretability

A smaller number of sources in this vault report an explicit judgment of overall slide or specimen readability, rather than a component-level measurement. Gundlapalle et al. (2022) reports a structured ordinal slide-quality grading approach, scored separately at the specimen periphery and center on a scale running from 1 (good) to a poorer grade reported as 5 (non-diagnostic), with pulsed-wave specimens graded better than continuous-wave specimens at both locations; the source does not describe this grading system as formally validated, and this review does not characterize it as such. Gobbo et al. (2017) reports a distinct kind of finding: in 21.5% of its specimens, thermal damage was too extensive or too subtle to measure, yet the source states that correct histological sampling and diagnosis were achieved in every case. This is evidence that the measurability of one damage metric and the overall usability of a specimen are not the same construct — inability to quantify thermal damage did not, in this dataset, prevent diagnosis. This finding is therefore interpreted here as evidence that thermal-damage measurability and specimen usability are not identical constructs, rather than as evidence that thermal damage is unrelated to interpretability more broadly. Seoane et al. (2013) raises a different kind of interpretability concern: pseudodysplastic artifact, a thermally induced pattern of cellular and nuclear change that the source states can be mistaken for genuine epithelial dysplasia. The source's own emphasis is on the risk of confusing this artifact with a true dysplastic border, assessed via a blinded two-pathologist consensus process, rather than on a formal numeric slide-quality score. These three sources assess different forms of interpretability — a graded slide-quality instrument, a measurability-versus-diagnosability divergence, and an interpretive risk of diagnostic confusion — and should not be collapsed into one common numeric scale.

Whole-specimen interpretability must also be distinguished from whether a diagnosis was ultimately rendered.

### 6.3 Interpretability versus diagnostic outcome

Diagnostic outcome — whether a diagnosis was ultimately rendered — is a coarser, binary or near-binary construct than specimen interpretability, and this review treats the two as distinct. Gobbo et al.'s own finding is the clearest demonstration within this vault: a specimen can remain diagnosable despite a measurement limitation severe enough that thermal damage itself could not be quantified. Accordingly, the presence of quantifiable artifact can remain methodologically relevant even when diagnosis was ultimately possible, as illustrated by Monteiro et al. (2019) and Vescovi et al. (2010). Angiero et al. (2011), by contrast, reports diagnostic outcome as directly size-graded, with diagnostic failure occurring in a substantial proportion of the smallest specimens in that series — a case where diagnostic outcome itself is the reported endpoint, without an accompanying interpretability or margin score. Diagnostic outcome indicates whether a diagnosis could ultimately be rendered, whereas specimen interpretability concerns the quality and readability of the histopathologic material through which that judgment is made. This review does not treat diagnostic success as proof that artifact is clinically irrelevant, does not treat interpretability and diagnostic outcome as interchangeable, and does not treat the absence of diagnostic failure in a given study as evidence of high specimen quality by itself; diagnostic outcome is retained as clinical or contextual evidence rather than as this review's primary ex vivo specimen-quality endpoint.

Interpretability is also shaped by specimen-level factors beyond laser exposure itself.

### 6.4 Specimen-level factors affecting vulnerability

Specimen size recurs in this vault's clinical literature as a factor associated with vulnerability to thermal-edge artifact and diagnostic limitation, independent of the exposure variables addressed in Sections 3 and 4. Angiero et al. (2011) reports diagnostic limitation as a function of specimen size within its own series, with unreadable/non-diagnostic outcomes concentrated among its smallest tested specimens and none at its largest. Vescovi et al. (2010), in a differently designed, non-diode study, reports smaller specimens as significantly more likely to show thermal artifact than larger ones within its own sample. Monteiro et al. (2019), by contrast, reports no significant correlation between specimen size and thermal-damage extension in its own dataset. These three findings are preserved here as study-specific results rather than merged into one relationship: Angiero's and Vescovi's size thresholds are not the same value, are drawn from different devices and tissue contexts, and Monteiro's null finding is a genuine result from a third, separate sample rather than a contradiction to be resolved. Across the reviewed studies, specimen size was associated with vulnerability in some study-specific contexts, whereas Monteiro et al. reported no corresponding size correlation; these findings do not support a single universal threshold. This review does not derive or recommend a clinical cutoff from these sources.

This reinforces why specimen-quality outcomes should be interpreted in relation to both laser exposure and specimen context.

### 6.5 Implications for outcome selection in ex vivo studies

Taken together, Sections 5 and 6 point toward a defensible hierarchy of outcome types for an ex vivo oral soft-tissue design, ordered from most directly measurable to most clinically contingent: objective thermal-injury magnitude, structured artifact-severity scoring, margin or incision condition, specimen interpretability or readability where it can be explicitly assessed, and diagnostic outcome treated as contextual rather than primary. An ex vivo design of the kind this review's evidence base draws on can assess histologic damage magnitude, artifact severity, tissue-architecture preservation, margin condition, and, where a structured instrument is used, specimen interpretability. It cannot directly establish clinical diagnostic yield, recurrence, healing, or other patient-level outcomes, since these depend on a real lesion and a living patient that a non-lesional ex vivo model does not include. Outcome selection for such a design should preserve the construct separation established across Section 5 and this section, rather than collapsing thermal magnitude, artifact severity, margin condition, and interpretability into a single composite score; no such composite is proposed here, and none of the reviewed sources defines one.

Specimen-level vulnerability, particularly specimen size, therefore warrants separate consideration.
